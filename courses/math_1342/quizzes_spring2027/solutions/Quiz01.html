Quiz 1

Data, Sampling, and Study Design

Worked Solutions

Covers Sections Intro, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3

Instructions. This is a take-home quiz. You may use your lecture notes and online resources, but the work you submit must be your own. Show your work clearly to receive credit.

StatKey problems. Problems marked STATKEY are done in StatKey (lock5stat.com/StatKey). Let D = the last digit of your ACC student ID, and use the row of each table that matches your D. For every StatKey problem, upload a screenshot of your own StatKey run that shows the graph and the summary panel (number of samples, mean, standard error, and any tail or percentile values you used). The numbers you report must match your screenshot. Answers that do not match your D or your screenshot receive no credit.

Total: 10 points

Problem 1 [2 pts]

For each variable, decide whether it is categorical or quantitative. (0.5 pt each)

  1. Zip code where a student lives
  2. Commute time to campus (minutes)
  3. Blood type
  4. Number of credit hours a student is taking

(a) Categorical — zip codes are labels; averaging them is meaningless. (b) Quantitative. (c) Categorical. (d) Quantitative.

Problem 2 [2 pts]

Match each scenario to the most appropriate sampling method (simple random, stratified, cluster, or convenience). (0.5 pt each)

  1. A reporter interviews the first 25 shoppers leaving a grocery store.
  2. A college numbers all 1,200 online students, and a computer picks 80 numbers at random.
  3. A district randomly selects 6 of its 40 schools and surveys every teacher at those 6 schools.
  4. A company splits employees into full-time and part-time groups and randomly selects 30 from each group.

(a) Convenience. (b) Simple random sample. (c) Cluster. (d) Stratified.

Problem 3 [2 pts]

A researcher surveys 300 adults and finds that those who drink more coffee report sleeping fewer hours per night. (1 pt each)

  1. Is this an experiment or an observational study? Explain.
  2. Name a possible confounding variable and explain how it could create this association without coffee causing less sleep.

(a) Observational study — the researcher did not assign how much coffee anyone drinks; she only recorded it.

(b) Example: work schedule / stress. People with long or stressful work hours may both drink more coffee and sleep less, so the association could be due to the work schedule rather than the coffee. (Other reasonable confounders, such as having young children or age, earn credit if the link to both variables is explained.)

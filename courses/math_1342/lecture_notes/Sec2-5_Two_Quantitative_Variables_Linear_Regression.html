Section 2.5

Linear Regression

Two quantitative variables: fitting a line to predict a response from an explanatory variable, interpreting its slope and intercept, and measuring residuals.

What Is Regression?

Linear regression finds the straight line that best fits the data in a scatterplot.

Goal

  • Find a line to predict the response variable \(y\) from the explanatory variable \(x\).
  • The line of best fit is called the regression line.
  • Notation: \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\). The hat means "predicted."

Running Example: Studying and Exam Scores

Can hours studied predict a student's exam score?
\[ \widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours} \]
Explanatory variable: hours studied. Response variable: exam score (points).

A Quick Review: Graphing Lines

A regression line is just a line — the same \(y = mx + b\) you met in algebra.

Slope–Intercept Form

\[ y = mx + b \]
  • \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept: the height where the line crosses the \(y\)-axis (when \(x = 0\)).
  • \(m\) is the slope: \(\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}\).
  • To graph: start at \((0, b)\), then use the slope to step to the next point.

Example: \(y = 1 + 2x\)

Intercept \(b = 1\) (crosses the \(y\)-axis at 1). Slope \(m = 2\): up 2 for every 1 step right.

Slope and Intercept

The two numbers that define the regression line \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\).

Slope \(b_1\)

  • The change in the predicted \(y\) for every one-unit increase in \(x\).
  • Positive slope: \(\hat{y}\) goes up as \(x\) increases. Negative slope: \(\hat{y}\) goes down.
  • Example: the predicted score rises by 5 points for each additional hour studied.

Intercept \(b_0\)

  • The predicted \(y\) value when \(x = 0\).
  • Example: a student who studies 0 hours has a predicted score of 50 points.
  • Often not meaningful if \(x = 0\) is far outside the range of the data.

Interpreting Slope & Intercept in Context

Don't just report the numbers — say what they mean, and attach the right units. Recall \(\widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours}\).

Slope: \(b_1 = 5\)

  • For each additional hour studied, the predicted exam score goes up by about 5 points.
  • Units of slope \(= \dfrac{\text{units of } y}{\text{units of } x} = \dfrac{\text{points}}{\text{hour}}\).

Intercept: \(b_0 = 50\)

  • When hours studied \(= 0\), the predicted exam score is 50 points.
  • Units of intercept \(=\) units of \(y = \) points.
  • Interpret with care: only meaningful if \(x = 0\) is a realistic value near the data.
Units rule of thumb: the slope is measured in \(y\)-units per \(x\)-unit, and the intercept is measured in \(y\)-units.

Making Predictions

Plug an \(x\)-value into the equation to get a predicted \(y\)-value, \(\hat{y}\).

Example
A student studies for 6 hours:
\[ \hat{y} = 50 + 5(6) = 80 \text{ points} \]
Another
A student studies for 3 hours:
\[ \hat{y} = 50 + 5(3) = 65 \text{ points} \]
A prediction is only as good as the data behind it. The 6-hour and 3-hour predictions are reasonable because students in the data studied between 1 and 10 hours.

Residuals

A residual measures how far the observed value is from the predicted value.

Definition

\[ \text{Residual} = y - \hat{y} = \text{observed} - \text{predicted} \]
Geometrically, it is the vertical distance from the point to the line. Positive: the point is above the line. Negative: below.

Least Squares Line

  • The regression line minimizes the sum of squared residuals.
  • This is why it is called the least squares line.
  • We rely on technology to find it.

Example: Computing a Residual

Using \(\widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours}\). Suppose a student studies 6 hours and actually scores 86 points.

Step 1 — Predict

\[ \hat{y} = 50 + 5(6) = 80 \text{ points} \]

Step 2 — Subtract

\[ y - \hat{y} = 86 - 80 = 6 \text{ points} \]

What It Means

  • The residual is \(+6\) points.
  • It is positive, so the observed point sits above the line.
  • The model under-predicted: the student scored 6 points higher than the line said.
  • Units of a residual match \(y\): points.
A negative residual would mean the point is below the line and the model over-predicted.

Seeing the Least Squares Line

Hours studied and exam score for 20 students. The line \(\widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours}\) makes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible.

Reading the Picture

  • Points above the line have positive residuals; points below have negative residuals.
  • The residuals always add to zero for the least squares line.
  • Any other line would make the sum of squared residuals larger.

Regression Cautions

Four important warnings when using regression.

Do Not Extrapolate

  • Do not predict far beyond the range of your data.
  • If \(x = 0\) is not near your data, the intercept is meaningless.

Check Your Assumptions

  • Only use the line if the relationship is approximately linear.
  • Outliers can be very influential on the regression line.
  • It matters which variable is explanatory and which is response.
Always plot your data! Technology will compute a regression line for any two quantitative variables, even if the relationship is not linear.

What to Remember

Regression gives a prediction equation; use it wisely.

Core Ideas

  • \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\)
  • Slope: change in \(\hat{y}\) per unit change in \(x\)
  • Intercept: \(\hat{y}\) when \(x = 0\)
  • Residual: \(y - \hat{y}\)
  • Least squares minimizes \(\sum (\text{residuals})^2\)

Cautions

  • Do not extrapolate.
  • Always plot your data.
  • Watch for influential outliers.
  • Correlation and regression do not prove causation.

Section 6.2

Inference for a Mean

Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for one population mean.

From z to t

For means, the main new issue is that the population standard deviation is usually unknown.

If the Statistic Is Normal

  • A confidence interval still has the form Statistic \(\pm z^* \cdot SE\).
  • A test statistic still compares the sample to the null in standard error units.
  • If we knew \(\sigma\), we could use the standard normal distribution.
\(t\)
Because \(\sigma\) is unknown, we replace it with \(s\). That changes the reference distribution (the curve for cutoffs and p-values) from \(z\) to \(t\), a bell curve like \(z\) but with heavier tails.

Standard Error for a Mean

The standard error measures how much sample means vary from sample to sample.

Population Formula

\[ SE(\bar{x})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]
This is the true standard error, but it requires the population standard deviation \(\sigma\).

What We Actually Use

\[ SE(\bar{x})=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]
Replacing \(\sigma\) with \(s\) adds uncertainty, so we use a t-distribution instead of a normal distribution.

When Can We Use t?

The t procedures work when the sample is large, or when the data are approximately normal.

\(n \ge 30\)
For large samples, the CLT makes the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) approximately normal.
\(df=n-1\)
The t-distribution depends on degrees of freedom: \(df=n-1\) picks which t curve to use. Higher \(df\) makes \(t\) closer to normal.
Small \(n\)
If the sample is small, only use t when the data look reasonably symmetric with no extreme outliers.
t vs z
As the degrees of freedom increase, the \(t\)-distribution gets closer to the standard normal distribution.

The t-Distribution

When the population SD is unknown, we use the t-distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. It has slightly heavier tails (more area far from 0) than the standard normal; for large \(n\) the two are almost identical.

~2 min Meet the Critter

The Gribble: A Tiny Crustacean That Eats Piers

  • Gribbles (genus Limnoria) are marine isopods (small, flat-bodied crustaceans), cousins of the pill bug. Most are only 1–4 mm long.
  • They tunnel into wood and eat it. After shipworms (wood-boring clams), they are the second-worst destroyers of wooden piers and jetties.
  • Unusual trick: a gribble digests wood with its own enzymes (proteins that break down food) in a nearly sterile gut. Termites, by contrast, rely on microbes in their guts.
  • Scientists at the University of York study those enzymes as a way to turn wood and straw into biofuel.
Why it matters: the next slides estimate the mean length of gribbles from a sample of 50.
Sources: “Gribble,” Wikipedia; University of York, Centre for Novel Agricultural Products; Kern et al., PNAS (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences), 2013. Photo: Limnoria quadripunctata, male and female.

Confidence Interval for a Mean

For a one-sample mean, use the sample mean, \(t^*\), and the standard error based on \(s\).

\[ \bar{x} \pm t^* \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]
  • \(t^*\) comes from a t-distribution with \(df=n-1\).
  • Use this when the sample is large or the data are approximately normal.
  • The margin of error is \(t^* \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\).

Gribbles Example Setup

  • Sample size: \(n=50\)
  • Sample mean: \(\bar{x}=3.1\) mm
  • Sample standard deviation: \(s=0.72\) mm
  • Goal: a \(90\%\) confidence interval for the average gribble length

Confidence Interval Example

A sample of \(50\) gribbles has average length \(3.1\) mm with standard deviation \(0.72\) mm.

Step 1
Find the degrees of freedom and \(t^*\) for a \(90\%\) confidence interval.
\[ df=50-1=49,\qquad t^* \approx 1.677 \]
Step 2
Compute the standard error.
\[ SE=\frac{0.72}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.102 \]
Step 3
Compute the margin of error.
\[ ME=1.677(0.102)\approx 0.171 \]
Step 4
Build the interval around the sample mean.
\[ 3.1 \pm 0.171=(2.929,\ 3.271) \]
Interpretation: We are \(90\%\) confident that the average length of gribbles is between \(2.929\) mm and \(3.271\) mm.

Hypothesis Testing for a Mean

For a mean, the test statistic uses a t-distribution with \(df=n-1\).

Test Statistic

\[ t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}} \]
The numerator compares the sample mean to the null mean \(\mu_0\).

p-Value

  • The p-value is the area in the tail(s) beyond the observed \(t\).
  • Use a t-distribution with \(df=n-1\).
  • The tail direction comes from the alternative hypothesis.
~2 min Meet the Study

The 1,000 Chips Challenge

  • In the 1990s, Nabisco advertised Chips Ahoy! cookies as “1000 chips delicious”: at least 1,000 chocolate chips in every bag.
  • In 1996, a third-grade class in Wadesboro, North Carolina counted chips for a math lesson, found as few as 340 per bag, and wrote to complain.
  • Nabisco staged a public recount: after dissolving the cookies in water to free the hidden chips, it counted 1,181 chips.
  • In 1999 the company dared the public to check the claim. Statistics students at the U.S. Air Force Academy counted the chips in 42 bags.
Why it matters: is the mean really above 1,000? That is a one-tail test for a mean, coming up next.
Sources: Mental Floss, “12 Facts About Chips Ahoy!”; Mashed, “The Surprising Showdown Between Chips Ahoy! and Third Graders.” Photo shows homemade cookies, not Chips Ahoy!

Hypothesis Test Example

Chips Ahoy! advertised at least \(1000\) chocolate chips per bag. A sample of \(42\) bags was counted.

Step 1
Set up the hypotheses for checking whether the average number of chips is greater than \(1000\).
\[ H_0:\mu=1000 \qquad H_a:\mu>1000 \]
Step 2
Check conditions and record the sample statistics: \(n=42\), \(\bar{x}=1261.6\), and \(s=117.6\).
With \(n=42\), the sample size is large, so the t test is appropriate.
Step 3
\[ t=\frac{1261.6-1000}{117.6/\sqrt{42}} \approx 14.42 \]
Steps 4-5
Find the right-tail p-value from a t-distribution with \(df=41\), then interpret in context.
\[ \text{p-value}\approx 0 \quad (\text{far less than } 0.0001) \]
Conclusion: We reject the null hypothesis \(H_0\). We have statistically significant evidence that the average number of chocolate chips per bag is greater than \(1000\).

Your Turn: Testing a Mean

A juice company labels its bottles as containing \(500\) mL. A consumer group measures a random sample of \(25\) bottles and finds \(\bar{x} = 488\) mL with \(s = 30\) mL. The data show no strong skew or outliers. Is there evidence that the bottles are underfilled on average?

Questions

  • (a) State \(H_0\) and \(H_a\).
  • (b) Compute the standard error and the t statistic.
  • (c) Use StatKey (t-distribution, \(df = 24\)) to find the p-value.
  • (d) State a conclusion at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Reminder

\[ t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad df=n-1 \]
\(n = 25 < 30\), so we rely on the data being roughly symmetric with no outliers.
Your Turn — Answers

Answers: Testing a Mean

Left-tail test with \(df = 25 - 1 = 24\).

(a) Hypotheses
\[ H_0:\mu=500 \qquad H_a:\mu<500 \]
(b) SE and t
\[ SE=\frac{30}{\sqrt{25}}=6 \qquad t=\frac{488-500}{6}=-2.00 \]
(c) p-Value
StatKey: t with df = 24, Left Tail, type \(-2.00\).
\[ \text{p-value}\approx 0.028 \]
(d) Conclusion
\(0.028 < 0.05\), so reject \(H_0\). There is evidence that the bottles contain less than 500 mL on average.

What to Remember

For means, the big difference is replacing \(\sigma\) with \(s\), which leads to the t-distribution.

Confidence Interval

\[ \bar{x} \pm t^* \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]
  • Use \(df=n-1\).
  • Need a large sample or approximately normal data.
  • Interpret the interval as a plausible range for the population mean.

Hypothesis Test

\[ t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}} \]
  • Use a t-distribution with \(df=n-1\).
  • The p-value is the tail area beyond the observed \(t\).
  • Small p-values give evidence against \(H_0\).

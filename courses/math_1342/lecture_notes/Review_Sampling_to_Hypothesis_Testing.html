  • Random sampling makes the sample look like the population.
  • Random assignment is what buys you cause and effect.

2. Describe (Ch. 2)

  • Shape, center, spread; graphs before formulas.
  • Produce a statistic: \(\hat{p},\ \bar{x},\ \bar{d},\ \hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2,\ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\).

3. Infer (Ch. 3–7)

  • Confidence interval: what is the parameter?
  • Hypothesis test: is this claimed value believable?
A statistic describes your sample. A parameter describes the population. Inference is the whole business of using the first to make a claim about the second — and the gap between them is exactly what SE measures.

From Population to p-value

Every sample gives a different statistic. Collect them and you get a sampling distribution — and that one distribution is what both confidence intervals and p-values are built from.

This is the whole semester. From test to test the only things that change are the statistic you compute and the SE formula. The picture never changes — which is why one framework handles all six tests.

Before Any Formula: Where the Numbers Came From

The design of the study decides what your p-value is allowed to mean. This is the part students skip and lose points on.

Who can I generalize to?

  • Random sample from the population → conclusions extend to that population.
  • Biased sample (volunteers, convenience, low response) → conclusions extend to nobody.
  • A bigger biased sample is not a fix. It just makes a wrong answer look precise.

Can I say one thing caused the other?

  • Randomized experiment (researcher assigns treatments at random) → yes, causation.
  • Observational study → association only. Confounding variables are always available as an alternative explanation.
  • No amount of statistical significance upgrades an observational study to a causal one.
Order of operations for every problem today: identify the population, the sample, and the design before you touch a formula. A perfect calculation on a biased sample is still worthless.

The Engine: Sampling Distribution and SE

Statistics vary from sample to sample. Quantifying that variation is what makes inference possible.

Sampling distribution

  • The distribution of a statistic over all possible samples of size \(n\).
  • Centered at the parameter.
  • Roughly normal when \(n\) is big enough — that is the CLT.

Standard error

  • The spread of that distribution: typical distance from statistic to parameter.
  • Shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\): quadruple \(n\) to halve the SE.
  • Every formula on the next slides is just a different SE.

Two ways to use it

  • CI: statistic \(\pm\) (critical value)(SE). Centered at the statistic.
  • Test: how many SEs is the statistic from the null value? Centered at the null value.
A p-value is a probability computed inside a world where \(H_0\) is true. That is why the test's distribution is centered at the null value and not at your data.

One Formula Runs Almost Every Test

There are six tests on your exam. Five of them are this single idea with a different SE.

\[ \text{test statistic}=\frac{\text{sample statistic}-\text{null value}}{SE} \]

What it means in words

  • "My sample came out this many standard errors away from what \(H_0\) claimed."
  • Far away (big \(|z|\) or \(|t|\)) → small p-value → \(H_0\) looks wrong.
  • Close to 0 → large p-value → ordinary sampling variation explains it.

Only four things change

  • The statistic (\(\hat{p}\), \(\bar{x}\), \(\bar{d}\), a difference).
  • The SE formula.
  • The distribution: \(z\) for proportions, \(t\) for means.
  • The df, when it is a \(t\).
The sixth test, \(\chi^2\), is the same instinct in different clothing: add up how far every observed count sits from the count \(H_0\) expects.

Choosing the Right Test

Two questions pick the test every time: how many variables, and what type? Then: are the two groups linked?

The trap: two columns of numbers with the same \(n\) is not automatically paired data. Ask whether each value in column 1 is tied to one specific value in column 2 (same person, same car, same married couple). If yes → paired \(t\). If no → two-sample \(t\).

Conditions and Tails

Know these cold — the conditions are worth points, and the tail is decided by the wording, never by the data.

Conditions to check out loud

  • Proportions: random sample, and the relevant counts are at least 10. For a test use \(np_0\ge 10\) and \(n(1-p_0)\ge 10\); for a \(2\times\) table, all four cell counts \(\ge 10\).
  • Means: random sample, and \(n\ge 30\) or the data are roughly normal with no strong outliers.
  • Paired: the same conditions, applied to the \(n_d\) differences.
  • \(\chi^2\): random sample, and every expected count is at least 5.

Picking the tail from the wording

  • "more than," "increases," "higher," "better" → \(H_a: >\), right tail.
  • "less than," "decreases," "lower," "reduces" → \(H_a: <\), left tail.
  • "differs from," "changed," "is there a difference" → \(H_a: \neq\), both tails, so double the tail area.
  • \(H_0\) always gets the equal sign, and always names a specific number (\(p_0\), \(\mu_0\), or 0 for a difference).
Choose the tail from the research question before you look at which direction your sample went.

Every Test, Same Six Steps

This is also the grading rubric for today's activity and for the final.

  1. Set the scene. Population, sample, design. Define the parameter in symbols and in words.
  2. Hypotheses. \(H_0\) with the equal sign, \(H_a\) from the wording. State \(\alpha\).
  3. Name the test and check conditions. Show the numbers, do not just claim it.
  1. Compute. Statistic, SE, then \(z\), \(t\), or \(\chi^2\). Report \(df\) if you used one.
  2. p-value. Tail area beyond your statistic, doubled if \(H_a\) is two-sided.
  3. Decide and conclude in context. Compare to \(\alpha\), then one sentence about the actual variables — no symbols.
Conclusion template. Reject: "Since \(p = \underline{\hphantom{0.000}} \le 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is statistically significant evidence that (the claim in \(H_a\), in plain words)." Do not reject: "Since \(p = \underline{\hphantom{0.000}} > 0.05\), we do not reject \(H_0\). There is not enough evidence to conclude that (the claim in \(H_a\))."

Saying It Right

Three places where correct arithmetic still gets the wrong answer.

What a p-value is

The probability of getting a sample statistic at least this extreme if \(H_0\) were true.
  • It is not \(P(H_0\text{ is true})\).
  • It is not the probability you made a mistake.
  • Small p-value = my data would be a weird coincidence under \(H_0\).

Never prove \(H_0\)

  • "Do not reject" means not enough evidence, not "\(H_0\) is true."
  • A real difference can hide behind a small sample.
  • Courtroom version: "not guilty" is not the same as "innocent."

Errors and importance

  • Type I: reject a true \(H_0\). Probability \(=\alpha\).
  • Type II: fail to reject a false \(H_0\).
  • Lower \(\alpha\) trades Type I risk for Type II risk. Bigger \(n\) lowers both.
  • Significant \(\neq\) large. A huge \(n\) makes trivial effects significant — look at the CI for the size.
Worked Example — We Do This One Together

Step 1: Read the Problem and Set the Scene

Before any formula, answer three questions: who is the population, how was the sample taken, and what number am I trying to learn about?

The problem

Student government claims that students at this college spend an average of $300 per semester on textbooks. A survey of 64 randomly selected students at the college found:

\[\bar{x}=\$272, \qquad s=\$96, \qquad n=64\]

Is there evidence that the mean amount spent differs from $300? Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Step 1 — the scene

  • Population: all students at this college.
  • Sample: 64 randomly selected students from that population.
  • Design: an observational survey — nobody was assigned to spend a certain amount.
  • Parameter: \(\mu=\) the mean textbook spending per semester for all students at this college.
\(\bar{x}=272\) is a fact about 64 students. \(\mu\) is the unknown we are actually arguing about. Keeping those two straight is most of this course.
Worked Example

Steps 2 and 3: Hypotheses, Then Name the Test

The wording sets the tail. The data type sets the test.

Step 2 — hypotheses

\[H_0: \mu=300 \qquad H_a: \mu\neq 300\]
  • \(H_0\) gets the equal sign and the claimed value, $300.
  • The question says "differs from" → two-tailed.
  • \(\alpha=0.05\), stated up front.
Do not take the bait. Our sample came out low (\$272), but that is not a reason to switch to \(H_a:\mu<300\). The tail comes from the question, which was asked before anyone saw the data.

Step 3 — test and conditions

How I know which test: one quantitative variable (dollars), one group, and I have \(s\) rather than \(\sigma\). That is a one-mean \(t\) test.

  • Random sample? Yes, stated. ✓
  • Normal enough? \(n=64\ge 30\), so the CLT makes the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) approximately normal. ✓
That second condition is doing real work here: textbook spending is almost certainly right-skewed, with a few students buying nothing and a few spending a fortune. We are not claiming the data are normal — only that \(\bar{x}\) is, because \(n\) is large.
Worked Example

Step 4: Compute the Test Statistic

Standard error first, then the one ratio that runs every test.

The arithmetic

\[SE=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{96}{\sqrt{64}}=\frac{96}{8}=12\]
\[t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{SE}=\frac{272-300}{12}=\frac{-28}{12}\approx -2.33\]
\[df=n-1=64-1=63\]

What each piece is telling us

  • \(SE=12\): if we repeated this survey over and over, sample means would typically land about \$12 away from the truth.
  • \(t=-2.33\): our sample mean sits \(2.33\) standard errors below the claimed \$300. The negative sign means below — it is information, not an error.
  • \(df=63\): which \(t\) curve to use. With \(df\) this large the curve is nearly standard normal, but the correct procedure is still \(t\), because \(\sigma\) is unknown.
Common slip: dividing by \(s=96\) instead of by \(SE=12\). The denominator is always the standard error, never the standard deviation.
Worked Example

Steps 5 and 6: p-value, Decision, Conclusion

Two tails, one comparison, then a sentence with dollars in it.

Step 5 — the p-value

\[P(T_{63}<-2.33)\approx 0.0114\]
\[p\text{-value}=2(0.0114)\approx \mathbf{0.0228}\]
  • \(H_a\) is two-sided, so we double the tail area — a result \$28 above \$300 would have been just as surprising.
  • In words: if the true mean really were \$300, we would see a sample mean this far off in either direction only about 2.3% of the time.

Step 6 — decide and say it

\[0.0228 \le 0.05 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{reject } H_0\]

Conclusion in context: Since \(p=0.0228\le 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is statistically significant evidence that the mean textbook spending for students at this college differs from \$300. Because \(\bar{x}=\$272\) is below \$300, the evidence points to students spending less than claimed.

Notice the sentence has dollars and students in it, not a single Greek letter. That is what "in context" means.
Worked Example

Finish the Job: Design, Size, and a Cross-Check

The test said "not \$300." It did not say why, and it did not say how much. Both matter.

The design question

  • Random sample of this college's students → we may generalize to this college.
  • Not to students everywhere — other colleges have different courses and prices.
  • Observational, so no cause and effect. Nothing here explains why spending is below \$300.

How much? Build the CI

\[272 \pm 2.00(12)=272\pm 24\]
\[(\$248,\ \$296)\]

Using \(t^*\approx 2.00\) for 95% with \(df=63\). We are 95% confident the true mean is between \$248 and \$296.

The two agree

  • \$300 is outside \((248,296)\).
  • So a two-tailed test at \(\alpha=0.05\) must reject \$300 — and it did.
  • The test answers "is it \$300?" The interval answers "then what is it?"
If your CI and your test ever disagree, you have made an arithmetic error. Use this as a free check.
Four ways to lose points on this problem. Forgetting to double the tail area (\(0.0114\) is not the answer). Dividing by \(s=96\) instead of \(SE=12\). Switching to a one-sided \(H_a\) after noticing \(\bar{x}<300\). And writing "the mean is \$272" — that is the sample mean, and it is exactly the thing we do not get to claim about the population.
Pair Activity

Your Turn: One Complete Test, With a Partner

Ten problems, one per pair. Pair up, take the problem number you are assigned, and work it all the way through on paper. Then each pair walks the class through their problem in about five minutes.

Do all seven of these

  1. Population, sample, and study design.
  2. Parameter in symbols and in words.
  3. \(H_0\) and \(H_a\), with the tail justified from the wording.
  4. Name the test and check every condition with numbers.
  5. Statistic, SE, test statistic, and \(df\) if needed.
  6. p-value, then decision at \(\alpha=0.05\).
  7. Conclusion in context — plus: does this design let you claim cause and effect?

How to work it

  • One of you narrates the reasoning, the other writes and checks the arithmetic — then swap at step 4.
  • Before computing anything, both of you say out loud which of the six tests it is and why. Most lost points live in that sentence.
  • If you disagree about the tail, reread the question's last sentence. The question wording, not your sample, sets the tail.
  • Round SE to at least 4 decimals so your test statistic lands where mine did.
  • Finish early? Build the matching 95% confidence interval and check that it agrees with your two-sided decision.
You have about 12 minutes. Answers follow the problem slides — do not peek until your pair has a conclusion written down.

Answer Key

One slide per problem. Each pair presents theirs. Watch for the design question at the bottom of every slide — that is the part that connects Chapter 1 to Chapter 7.

Answer — Problem 1

One-Proportion \(z\) Test, Right-Tailed

Random sample of 300 students; 198 work. Claim: more than 60% work.

Setup

Population: all students at the college. Sample: 300 randomly selected students. Parameter: \(p=\) the proportion of all students at the college who work while enrolled.

\[H_0: p=0.60 \qquad H_a: p>0.60\]

"More than 60%" is a right-tailed alternative.

Test and conditions

One-proportion \(z\) test. Random sample ✓. \(np_0=300(0.60)=180\ge 10\) ✓, \(n(1-p_0)=300(0.40)=120\ge 10\) ✓.

Compute

\[\hat{p}=\frac{198}{300}=0.66\]
\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.60(0.40)}{300}}=0.0283\]
\[z=\frac{0.66-0.60}{0.0283}\approx 2.12\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(Z>2.12)\approx \mathbf{0.0169}\).

Since \(0.0169\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is statistically significant evidence that more than 60% of the college's students work while enrolled.

Design: a random sample, so we can generalize to all students at this college. It is observational, so nothing here is a cause-and-effect claim — and it says nothing about students at other colleges.
Answer — Problem 2

One-Mean \(t\) Test, Two-Tailed

36 bags: \(\bar{x}=27.6\) g, \(s=1.2\) g. Label claims 28.0 g.

Setup

Population: all bags from this production process. Sample: 36 randomly selected bags. Parameter: \(\mu=\) the mean net weight of all bags.

\[H_0: \mu=28.0 \qquad H_a: \mu\neq 28.0\]

"Differs from" is two-tailed — underfilling and overfilling are both quality problems.

Test and conditions

One-mean \(t\) test. Random sample ✓. \(n=36\ge 30\), so the CLT makes the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) approximately normal ✓.

Compute

\[SE=\frac{1.2}{\sqrt{36}}=0.20\]
\[t=\frac{27.6-28.0}{0.20}=-2.00,\quad df=35\]

p-value and conclusion

Two-tailed: \(p\text{-value}=2\,P(T_{35}<-2.00)\approx 2(0.0266)= \mathbf{0.053}\).

Since \(0.053>0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\). There is not enough evidence to conclude that the mean net weight differs from 28.0 g.

Two things worth arguing about. (1) \(p=0.053\) is barely above \(\alpha\), and "do not reject" still does not mean the mean is 28.0 — a 95% CI would contain 28.0, but only just. (2) Had the inspector's question been "is the mean less than 28.0," the one-tailed p-value would be \(0.027\) and the decision would flip. That is exactly why the tail must be set by the question, not by the data.
Answer — Problem 3

Two-Proportion \(z\) Test, Two-Tailed

Campus A: 120 of 250. Campus B: 96 of 250.

Setup

Parameters: \(p_1,p_2=\) the proportion of all students at Campus A, Campus B who had a flu shot. Samples: independent random samples of 250 from each campus.

\[H_0: p_1=p_2 \qquad H_a: p_1\neq p_2\]

Test and conditions

Two-proportion \(z\) test. Independent random samples ✓. All four counts \(\ge 10\): \(120,\ 130,\ 96,\ 154\) ✓.

\(\hat{p}_1=\dfrac{120}{250}=0.480\), \(\hat{p}_2=\dfrac{96}{250}=0.384\), difference \(=0.096\).

Compute

A test of \(p_1=p_2\) uses the pooled proportion in the SE.

\[\hat{p}=\frac{120+96}{250+250}=\frac{216}{500}=0.432\]
\[SE=\sqrt{0.432(0.568)\left(\tfrac{1}{250}+\tfrac{1}{250}\right)}=0.0443\]
\[z=\frac{0.096}{0.0443}\approx 2.17\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=2\,P(Z>2.17)\approx \mathbf{0.030}\). Since \(0.030\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the two campuses differ in flu-shot rate.

Design: observational. The campuses were not assigned to students, so "which campus you attend" is not shown to cause the difference — the campuses may differ in age, program mix, or clinic access. Note the pooled SE is used here but not in a confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\).
Answer — Problem 4

Two-Sample \(t\) Test, Right-Tailed

Flashcards: \(n_1=40,\ \bar{x}_1=82.5,\ s_1=7.0\). Rereading: \(n_2=45,\ \bar{x}_2=78.9,\ s_2=8.0\).

Setup

Parameters: \(\mu_1,\mu_2=\) the mean final-exam score if students like these used flashcards, rereading. Design: a randomized experiment.

\[H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2 \qquad H_a: \mu_1>\mu_2\]

"Higher" is right-tailed. The two groups are separate students, so this is not paired — and \(40\neq 45\) confirms it.

Test and conditions

Two-sample \(t\) test. Random assignment ✓. \(n_1=40\) and \(n_2=45\) are both \(\ge 30\) ✓.

Compute

\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{7.0^2}{40}+\frac{8.0^2}{45}}=\sqrt{1.225+1.422}\approx 1.627\]
\[t=\frac{82.5-78.9}{1.627}\approx 2.21\]
\[df=\min(40-1,\ 45-1)=39\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(T_{39}>2.21)\approx \mathbf{0.016}\). Since \(0.016\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the mean exam score is higher with flashcards than with rereading.

Design: this one is a randomized experiment, so a causal conclusion is legitimate — flashcards caused the higher mean score for these students. Compare this to Problem 3, where identical arithmetic buys you only an association.
Answer — Problem 5

Paired \(t\) Test, Left-Tailed

25 runners, each timed twice. \(d=\) after \(-\) before, \(\bar{d}=-34.0\) s, \(s_d=68.0\) s.

Setup

Parameter: \(\mu_d=\) the mean change in 5K time (after \(-\) before) for runners like these. Why paired: the same runner is measured twice, so each "after" is tied to one specific "before."

\[H_0: \mu_d=0 \qquad H_a: \mu_d<0\]

"Reduces time" means after \(<\) before, so the differences should be negative — left-tailed.

Test and conditions

Paired \(t\) test, which is a one-mean \(t\) test on the \(n_d=25\) differences. Here \(n_d=25<30\), so we need the differences to be roughly normal with no strong outliers — state this as an assumption.

Compute

\[SE=\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n_d}}=\frac{68.0}{\sqrt{25}}=13.6\]
\[t=\frac{-34.0-0}{13.6}=-2.50,\quad df=24\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(T_{24}<-2.50)\approx \mathbf{0.010}\).

Since \(0.010\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that mean 5K time decreased, by about 34 seconds on average.

Design: significant, but not a randomized experiment — there is no control group. Every runner trained, so ordinary practice effects, familiarity with the course, or better weather on day two are all live explanations. The test establishes that times dropped; it does not establish that interval training is why.
Answer — Problem 6

\(\chi^2\) Test for Association

240 randomly selected students; commuter status by usual study location.

Setup and conditions

\(H_0\): there is no association between commuter status and study location.
\(H_a\): there is an association.

\(\chi^2\) test for association. Random sample ✓. Expected counts, \(E=\dfrac{\text{row total}\times\text{column total}}{n}\):

ExpectedLibraryHomeCafé
Commuter405030
On campus405030

Both rows total 120, so each row gets the same expected counts. Smallest expected count is 30, well above 5 ✓.

Compute

\[\chi^2=\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}\]
\[=\frac{(30-40)^2}{40}+\frac{(60-50)^2}{50}+0+\frac{(50-40)^2}{40}+\frac{(40-50)^2}{50}+0\]
\[=2.5+2.0+0+2.5+2.0+0=9.00\]
\[df=(2-1)(3-1)=2\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(\chi^2_2>9.00)\approx \mathbf{0.011}\). Since \(0.011\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence of an association between commuter status and where students study.

Then describe it. A \(\chi^2\) test says "related," never how. Compare \(O\) to \(E\): commuters study at home more than expected (60 vs 50) and use the library less (30 vs 40); on-campus students do the reverse. The café column matches expectation exactly and contributes nothing to \(\chi^2\). Observational data, so association only. The p-value is always the right tail, even though \(H_a\) has no direction.
Answer — Problem 7

One-Proportion \(z\) Test, Left-Tailed

Historical pass rate 75%. After the change: 210 of 300 passed.

Setup

Parameter: \(p=\) the proportion of all students who pass the course under the new curriculum. Sample: 300 randomly selected students.

\[H_0: p=0.75 \qquad H_a: p<0.75\]

"Dropped" is left-tailed. Note \(H_0\) uses the historical rate as the null value.

Test and conditions

One-proportion \(z\) test. Random sample ✓. \(np_0=300(0.75)=225\ge 10\) ✓, \(n(1-p_0)=300(0.25)=75\ge 10\) ✓.

Compute

\[\hat{p}=\frac{210}{300}=0.70\]
\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.75(0.25)}{300}}=0.0250\]
\[z=\frac{0.70-0.75}{0.0250}=-2.00\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(Z<-2.00)\approx \mathbf{0.0228}\).

Since \(0.0228\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the pass rate is now below 75%.

Design: this is a before-and-after comparison with no random assignment, so the curriculum change is not established as the cause. Different students, a different semester, and any other simultaneous change are all confounded with the new curriculum. The drop is real; the explanation is not settled.
Answer — Problem 8

One-Mean \(t\) Test, Right-Tailed

49 visits: \(\bar{x}=22.4\) min, \(s=8.4\) min. Advertised mean: 20 min.

Setup

Parameter: \(\mu=\) the mean wait time for all visits to this clinic. Sample: 49 randomly selected visits.

\[H_0: \mu=20 \qquad H_a: \mu>20\]

"Longer than advertised" is right-tailed.

Test and conditions

One-mean \(t\) test. Random sample ✓. \(n=49\ge 30\) ✓. This matters here: wait times are usually right-skewed, so we are leaning on the CLT rather than on normal data.

Compute

\[SE=\frac{8.4}{\sqrt{49}}=1.20\]
\[t=\frac{22.4-20}{1.20}=2.00,\quad df=48\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(T_{48}>2.00)\approx \mathbf{0.026}\).

Since \(0.026\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the mean wait time at this clinic is longer than the advertised 20 minutes.

Statistical vs practical significance. The estimated excess is about 2.4 minutes. That is statistically significant, but whether a 2.4-minute average overrun matters to patients is a judgment call, not a calculation. A confidence interval answers "how much?"; a test only answers "is it more than 20?"
Answer — Problem 9

Two-Sample \(t\) Test, Two-Tailed

First-year: \(n_1=32,\ \bar{x}_1=6.8,\ s_1=1.6\). Returning: \(n_2=38,\ \bar{x}_2=7.4,\ s_2=1.5\).

Setup

Parameters: \(\mu_1,\mu_2=\) mean weeknight sleep hours for all first-year, all returning students. Separate groups of students with no link between them, so not paired.

\[H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2 \qquad H_a: \mu_1\neq\mu_2\]

Test and conditions

Two-sample \(t\) test. Independent random samples ✓. \(n_1=32\) and \(n_2=38\) both \(\ge 30\) ✓.

Compute

\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{1.6^2}{32}+\frac{1.5^2}{38}}=\sqrt{0.0800+0.0592}\approx 0.3731\]
\[t=\frac{6.8-7.4}{0.3731}\approx -1.61,\quad df=\min(31,37)=31\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=2\,P(T_{31}<-1.61)\approx \mathbf{0.118}\).

Since \(0.118>0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\). There is not enough evidence to conclude that mean weeknight sleep differs between first-year and returning students.

Say this carefully. "Not enough evidence" is not "the two means are equal," and it is certainly not "first-year students sleep the same amount." A real 0.6-hour gap could easily exist with samples this size failing to detect it — that would be a Type II error. Larger samples would shrink the SE and could turn this same 0.6-hour difference significant.
Answer — Problem 10

Two-Proportion \(z\) Test, Right-Tailed

Reminder: 130 of 200 kept the appointment. No reminder: 108 of 200.

Setup

Parameters: \(p_1,p_2=\) the proportion who keep the appointment with a reminder, without a reminder. Design: a randomized experiment.

\[H_0: p_1=p_2 \qquad H_a: p_1>p_2\]

Test and conditions

Two-proportion \(z\) test. Random assignment ✓. All four counts \(\ge 10\): \(130,\ 70,\ 108,\ 92\) ✓.

\(\hat{p}_1=\dfrac{130}{200}=0.650\), \(\hat{p}_2=\dfrac{108}{200}=0.540\), difference \(=0.110\).

Compute

\[\hat{p}=\frac{130+108}{400}=\frac{238}{400}=0.595\]
\[SE=\sqrt{0.595(0.405)\left(\tfrac{1}{200}+\tfrac{1}{200}\right)}=0.0491\]
\[z=\frac{0.110}{0.0491}\approx 2.24\]

p-value and conclusion

\(p\text{-value}=P(Z>2.24)\approx \mathbf{0.013}\). Since \(0.013\le 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that text reminders increase the proportion of students who keep their advising appointment.

Design: random assignment, so this is a genuine causal claim — the reminders caused the increase, estimated at about 11 percentage points. This is the payoff of Chapter 1: same z-test machinery as Problem 3, but a stronger conclusion, purchased entirely by the design.

What to Carry Into the Final

If you remember six things from this semester, remember these.

Design first

  • Random sample → you may generalize.
  • Random assignment → you may claim cause.
  • Neither is fixed by a bigger \(n\) or a smaller p-value.

Name the test before computing

  • How many variables, and what type?
  • One group or two? If two, are they linked?
  • Proportions get \(z\); means get \(t\) with \(df\).

It is always one ratio

  • \(\dfrac{\text{statistic}-\text{null value}}{SE}\)
  • Only the SE and the distribution change.
  • \(\chi^2\) compares observed to expected counts.

Conditions earn points

  • Counts \(\ge 10\) for proportions.
  • \(n\ge 30\) or roughly normal for means.
  • Expected counts \(\ge 5\) for \(\chi^2\).

The tail comes from the words

  • Set \(H_a\) before looking at your sample.
  • "Differs" → two tails, so double the area.
  • \(\chi^2\) p-values are always right-tailed.

Conclude in context

  • Compare \(p\) to \(\alpha\), then write a sentence about the real variables.
  • Never "prove \(H_0\)" — only "not enough evidence."
  • Significant \(\neq\) important. Use a CI for size.

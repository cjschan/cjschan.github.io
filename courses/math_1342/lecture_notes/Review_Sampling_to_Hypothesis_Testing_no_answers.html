A statistic describes your sample. A parameter describes the population. Inference is the whole business of using the first to make a claim about the second — and the gap between them is exactly what SE measures.

Step 1: Where the Numbers Came From

The design of the study decides what your p-value is allowed to mean. This is the part students skip and lose points on.

Who can I generalize to?

  • Random sample from the population → conclusions extend to that population.
  • Biased sample (volunteers, convenience, low response) → conclusions extend to nobody.
  • A bigger biased sample is not a fix. It just makes a wrong answer look precise.

Can I say one thing caused the other?

  • Randomized experiment (researcher assigns treatments at random) → yes, causation.
  • Observational study → association only. Confounding variables are always available as an alternative explanation.
  • No amount of statistical significance upgrades an observational study to a causal one.
Order of operations for every problem today: identify the population, the sample, and the design before you touch a formula. A perfect calculation on a biased sample is still worthless.

The Engine: Sampling Distribution and SE

Statistics vary from sample to sample. Quantifying that variation is what makes inference possible.

Sampling distribution

  • The distribution of a statistic over all possible samples of size \(n\).
  • Centered at the parameter.
  • Roughly normal when \(n\) is big enough — that is the CLT.

Standard error

  • The spread of that distribution: typical distance from statistic to parameter.
  • Shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\): quadruple \(n\) to halve the SE.
  • Every formula on the next slides is just a different SE.

Two ways to use it

  • CI: statistic \(\pm\) (critical value)(SE). Centered at the statistic.
  • Test: how many SEs is the statistic from the null value? Centered at the null value.
A p-value is a probability computed inside a world where \(H_0\) is true. That is why the test's distribution is centered at the null value and not at your data.

One Formula Runs Almost Every Test

There are six tests on your exam. Five of them are this single idea with a different SE.

\[ \text{test statistic}=\frac{\text{sample statistic}-\text{null value}}{SE} \]

What it means in words

  • "My sample came out this many standard errors away from what \(H_0\) claimed."
  • Far away (big \(|z|\) or \(|t|\)) → small p-value → \(H_0\) looks wrong.
  • Close to 0 → large p-value → ordinary sampling variation explains it.

Only four things change

  • The statistic (\(\hat{p}\), \(\bar{x}\), \(\bar{d}\), a difference).
  • The SE formula.
  • The distribution: \(z\) for proportions, \(t\) for means.
  • The df, when it is a \(t\).
The sixth test, \(\chi^2\), is the same instinct in different clothing: add up how far every observed count sits from the count \(H_0\) expects.

Choosing the Right Test

Two questions pick the test every time: how many variables, and what type? Then: are the two groups linked?

What the problem gives youParameterTestStatistic & df
One categorical variable, one group (a count out of \(n\))\(p\)One-proportion \(z\)\(z=\dfrac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\)
One quantitative variable, one group (\(\bar{x}\), \(s\))\(\mu\)One-mean \(t\)\(t=\dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\), \(df=n-1\)
Two separate groups, categorical response\(p_1-p_2\)Two-proportion \(z\) (pooled)\(z=\dfrac{\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\)
Two separate groups, quantitative response\(\mu_1-\mu_2\)Two-sample \(t\)\(t=\dfrac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}\), \(df=\min(n_1-1,n_2-1)\)
Two quantitative measurements on the same unit (or matched pairs)\(\mu_d\)Paired \(t\)\(t=\dfrac{\bar{d}-0}{s_d/\sqrt{n_d}}\), \(df=n_d-1\)
Two categorical variables, an \(r\times c\) tableassociation\(\chi^2\) test for association\(\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E}\), \(df=(r-1)(c-1)\)
The trap: two columns of numbers with the same \(n\) is not automatically paired data. Ask whether each value in column 1 is tied to one specific value in column 2 (same person, same car, same married couple). If yes → paired \(t\). If no → two-sample \(t\).

Conditions and Tails

Know these cold — the conditions are worth points, and the tail is decided by the wording, never by the data.

Conditions to check out loud

  • Proportions: random sample, and the relevant counts are at least 10. For a test use \(np_0\ge 10\) and \(n(1-p_0)\ge 10\); for a \(2\times\) table, all four cell counts \(\ge 10\).
  • Means: random sample, and \(n\ge 30\) or the data are roughly normal with no strong outliers.
  • Paired: the same conditions, applied to the \(n_d\) differences.
  • \(\chi^2\): random sample, and every expected count is at least 5.

Picking the tail from the wording

  • "more than," "increases," "higher," "better" → \(H_a: >\), right tail.
  • "less than," "decreases," "lower," "reduces" → \(H_a: <\), left tail.
  • "differs from," "changed," "is there a difference" → \(H_a: \neq\), both tails, so double the tail area.
  • \(H_0\) always gets the equal sign, and always names a specific number (\(p_0\), \(\mu_0\), or 0 for a difference).
Choose the tail from the research question before you look at which direction your sample went.

Every Test, Same Six Steps

This is also the grading rubric for today's activity and for the final.

  1. Set the scene. Population, sample, design. Define the parameter in symbols and in words.
  2. Hypotheses. \(H_0\) with the equal sign, \(H_a\) from the wording. State \(\alpha\).
  3. Name the test and check conditions. Show the numbers, do not just claim it.
  1. Compute. Statistic, SE, then \(z\), \(t\), or \(\chi^2\). Report \(df\) if you used one.
  2. p-value. Tail area beyond your statistic, doubled if \(H_a\) is two-sided.
  3. Decide and conclude in context. Compare to \(\alpha\), then one sentence about the actual variables — no symbols.
Conclusion template. Reject: "Since \(p = \underline{\hphantom{0.000}} \le 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). There is statistically significant evidence that (the claim in \(H_a\), in plain words)." Do not reject: "Since \(p = \underline{\hphantom{0.000}} > 0.05\), we do not reject \(H_0\). There is not enough evidence to conclude that (the claim in \(H_a\))."

Saying It Right

Three places where correct arithmetic still gets the wrong answer.

What a p-value is

The probability of getting a sample statistic at least this extreme if \(H_0\) were true.
  • It is not \(P(H_0\text{ is true})\).
  • It is not the probability you made a mistake.
  • Small p-value = my data would be a weird coincidence under \(H_0\).

Never prove \(H_0\)

  • "Do not reject" means not enough evidence, not "\(H_0\) is true."
  • A real difference can hide behind a small sample.
  • Courtroom version: "not guilty" is not the same as "innocent."

Errors and importance

  • Type I: reject a true \(H_0\). Probability \(=\alpha\).
  • Type II: fail to reject a false \(H_0\).
  • Lower \(\alpha\) trades Type I risk for Type II risk. Bigger \(n\) lowers both.
  • Significant \(\neq\) large. A huge \(n\) makes trivial effects significant — look at the CI for the size.
Pair Activity

Your Turn: One Complete Test, With a Partner

Ten problems, one per pair. Pair up, take the problem number you are assigned, and work it all the way through on paper. Then each pair walks the class through their problem in about five minutes.

Do all seven of these

  1. Population, sample, and study design.
  2. Parameter in symbols and in words.
  3. \(H_0\) and \(H_a\), with the tail justified from the wording.
  4. Name the test and check every condition with numbers.
  5. Statistic, SE, test statistic, and \(df\) if needed.
  6. p-value, then decision at \(\alpha=0.05\).
  7. Conclusion in context — plus: does this design let you claim cause and effect?

How to work it

  • One of you narrates the reasoning, the other writes and checks the arithmetic — then swap at step 4.
  • Before computing anything, both of you say out loud which of the six tests it is and why. Most lost points live in that sentence.
  • If you disagree about the tail, reread the question's last sentence. The question wording, not your sample, sets the tail.
  • Round SE to at least 4 decimals so your test statistic lands where mine did.
  • Finish early? Build the matching 95% confidence interval and check that it agrees with your two-sided decision.
You have about 12 minutes. We will work through every problem together as a class afterward — have a written conclusion on paper before we do.

What to Carry Into the Final

If you remember six things from this semester, remember these.

Design first

  • Random sample → you may generalize.
  • Random assignment → you may claim cause.
  • Neither is fixed by a bigger \(n\) or a smaller p-value.

Name the test before computing

  • How many variables, and what type?
  • One group or two? If two, are they linked?
  • Proportions get \(z\); means get \(t\) with \(df\).

It is always one ratio

  • \(\dfrac{\text{statistic}-\text{null value}}{SE}\)
  • Only the SE and the distribution change.
  • \(\chi^2\) compares observed to expected counts.

Conditions earn points

  • Counts \(\ge 10\) for proportions.
  • \(n\ge 30\) or roughly normal for means.
  • Expected counts \(\ge 5\) for \(\chi^2\).

The tail comes from the words

  • Set \(H_a\) before looking at your sample.
  • "Differs" → two tails, so double the area.
  • \(\chi^2\) p-values are always right-tailed.

Conclude in context

  • Compare \(p\) to \(\alpha\), then write a sentence about the real variables.
  • Never "prove \(H_0\)" — only "not enough evidence."
  • Significant \(\neq\) important. Use a CI for size.

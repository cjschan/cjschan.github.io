Section 9.1

Inference for Slope and Correlation

Moving from describing a linear relationship in a sample to testing whether that relationship exists in the population: hypothesis tests and confidence intervals for the slope and the correlation.

From Describing to Inferring

In Sections 2.4 and 2.5 we summarized a sample with a correlation and a regression line. Now we ask what the sample tells us about the population.

What we did before (description)

  • Drew a scatterplot of two quantitative variables.
  • Computed the sample correlation \(r\).
  • Fit the least squares line \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\) and interpreted its slope.
  • All of these are statistics: they describe the sample we happened to collect.

What we do now (inference)

  • A different sample would give a different \(r\) and a different slope.
  • So a tilted line in our sample might just be chance.
  • Key question: is there evidence of a linear relationship in the population?
  • Tools: a hypothesis test and a confidence interval, both using a t-distribution.
Even when two variables are completely unrelated in the population, the sample slope is almost never exactly 0. Inference tells us whether our slope is far enough from 0 to be convincing.

The Simple Linear Model

Definitions: the population has a true line; the sample gives us an estimate of it.

Population model

\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon \]
  • \(\beta_0\) (beta-zero): the population intercept.
  • \(\beta_1\) (beta-one): the population slope, the true change in the mean of \(y\) when \(x\) increases by one unit.
  • \(\varepsilon\): random error, the scatter of individuals around the line.
  • \(\rho\) (rho): the population correlation between \(x\) and \(y\).

Sample estimates

\[ \hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x \]
  • \(b_0\) estimates \(\beta_0\); \(b_1\) estimates \(\beta_1\); \(r\) estimates \(\rho\).
  • Each estimate has a standard error that measures how much it would vary from sample to sample.
  • Parameters are Greek letters and fixed; statistics are Roman letters and change with the sample.
The central question of this section: is \(\beta_1 = 0\) (no linear relationship in the population) or is \(\beta_1 \neq 0\)? Equivalently, is \(\rho = 0\) or \(\rho \neq 0\)?

Why a Test? The Sampling Distribution of the Slope

Imagine taking many samples of 253 students. Each sample gives its own slope \(b_1\). If there were no relationship in the population, those slopes would pile up around 0.

Sampling distribution of \(b_1\) when \(\beta_1 = 0\), with standard error 0.0337. The shaded tails are the p-value for our observed slope.

Reading the picture

  • Center: 0, because \(H_0\) says the population slope is 0.
  • Spread: the standard error of the slope, \(SE_{b_1}\), reported by software.
  • Our sample slope, 0.0831, sits about 2.5 standard errors above 0.
  • The shaded area (both tails) is small, so a slope this far from 0 would be unusual if \(H_0\) were true.
\[ t = \frac{b_1 - 0}{SE_{b_1}} = \frac{0.0831}{0.0337} \approx 2.47 \]

Test for Correlation

Definition: a hypothesis test for whether the population correlation is zero.

Hypotheses

\[ H_0:\ \rho = 0 \qquad H_a:\ \rho \neq 0 \]
In words: \(H_0\) says there is no linear relationship between the two variables in the population. \(H_a\) says there is one. A one-sided alternative (\(\rho > 0\) or \(\rho < 0\)) is used when the direction is specified in advance.

Test statistic

\[ t = \frac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}, \qquad df = n - 2 \]
  • \(r\) is the sample correlation and \(n\) is the number of pairs.
  • Compare to a t-distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom to get the p-value.
  • For the sleep data: \(t = \dfrac{0.154\sqrt{251}}{\sqrt{1 - 0.154^2}} \approx 2.47\), p-value \(= 0.014\).
Why \(n - 2\)? Fitting a line uses up two pieces of information from the data, the intercept and the slope. Every t-procedure in this section uses \(df = n - 2\).

Test for Slope

Definition: a hypothesis test for whether the population slope is zero, which means whether \(x\) is a useful linear predictor of \(y\).

Hypotheses and statistic

\[ H_0:\ \beta_1 = 0 \qquad H_a:\ \beta_1 \neq 0 \]
\[ t = \frac{b_1 - 0}{SE_{b_1}}, \qquad df = n - 2 \]
\(b_1\) is the sample slope and \(SE_{b_1}\) is its standard error. Both are printed in the regression output, along with \(t\) and the p-value.

Same test, two names

  • "No linear relationship" can be written as \(\rho = 0\) or as \(\beta_1 = 0\). They say the same thing.
  • The t-statistic for the correlation and the t-statistic for the slope are always identical, and so are the p-values.
  • Use whichever version the output gives you; the conclusion is the same.
  • Reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is below the significance level; conclude there is evidence of a linear relationship.

Using the output on the previous slide, test whether depression score and classes missed are correlated for students at this college.

Question
(a) What is the sample correlation? (b) What is the p-value for testing the correlation? (c) Give the conclusion of the test in context.
Hypotheses
\[ H_0:\ \rho = 0 \qquad H_a:\ \rho \neq 0 \]
In words: \(H_0\) says depression score and classes missed are not linearly related for all students at this college; \(H_a\) says they are.
Answers
  • (a) The sample correlation is \(r = 0.154\), a weak positive association.
  • (b) The p-value for the correlation test is \(0.014\).
  • (c) At a 5% level, \(0.014 < 0.05\), so we reject \(H_0\). There is evidence that \(\rho \neq 0\): some association exists between depression scores and the number of classes missed.
Rejecting \(H_0\) says the relationship is real, not that it is strong. The correlation is only 0.154.

Confidence Interval for the Slope

Definition: a range of plausible values for the population slope \(\beta_1\), built the same way as every other t-interval.

Formula

\[ b_1 \pm t^* \cdot SE_{b_1}, \qquad df = n - 2 \]
  • \(b_1\) and \(SE_{b_1}\) come from the regression output.
  • \(t^*\) is the critical value from a t-distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom, chosen for the confidence level.
  • \(t^* \cdot SE_{b_1}\) is the margin of error.

Interpretation template

We are 95% confident that the slope of the line to predict [response] from [explanatory] for [population] is between [lower] and [upper].
  • The slope is in units of \(y\) per one unit of \(x\).
  • If the interval contains 0, the data do not rule out "no linear relationship." That matches not rejecting \(H_0\) in the test.
  • If 0 is outside the interval, the slope is significantly different from 0, matching a rejection of \(H_0\).

Example 1: A 95% Confidence Interval for the Slope

(h) What is the standard error of the slope? Find and interpret a 95% confidence interval for the slope of the regression line.

Answers
From the output, \(SE_{b_1} = 0.03368\). With \(df = n - 2 = 251\), the 95% critical value is \(t^* = 1.970\).
\[ \begin{aligned} b_1 \pm t^* \cdot SE_{b_1} &= 0.0831 \pm 1.970(0.03368) \\ &= 0.0831 \pm 0.0664 \\ &= 0.0167 \ \text{to}\ 0.1495 \end{aligned} \]

Interpretation

We are 95% sure that the slope of the linear model to predict classes missed from depression score, for all students at this college, is between 0.0167 and 0.1495.
  • Every plausible slope is positive: each extra point of depression score is associated with somewhere between about 0.02 and 0.15 more classes missed.
  • The interval does not contain 0, which agrees with rejecting \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) at the 5% level.
  • The interval is wide relative to the estimate because the relationship is weak and the data are noisy.

Example 1: Two p-values and \(R^2\)

(i) Compare the two p-values, from the test for correlation and the test for slope. (j) What is \(R^2\) for this model? Interpret it in context.

Answer (i)
0.014
Both the correlation test and the slope test give p-value 0.014. They are the same, as expected: both test "no linear relationship."

Definition: \(R^2\)

\[ R^2 = r^2 \]
The proportion of the variability in the response variable that is explained by the linear model. It is always between 0 and 1 (0% and 100%).
Answer (j)
2.4%
\(R^2 = 0.154^2 \approx 0.024\). Depression scores explain only 2.4% of the variability in the number of classes missed.
Statistically significant does not mean practically important. With \(n = 253\), even a weak relationship (\(r = 0.154\)) produces a small p-value. The tiny \(R^2\) reminds us that depression score alone is a poor predictor of classes missed.

Conditions for Inference on a Line

The t-procedures for slope and correlation rely on a few conditions. Check them with a scatterplot and a residual plot.

Conditions

  • Linearity. The scatterplot shows a roughly linear pattern, and the residual plot has no curve.
  • Constant variability. The vertical spread of residuals is about the same across all values of \(x\).
  • Normal residuals. Residuals are roughly symmetric and bell-shaped, with no extreme outliers. Less critical when \(n\) is large.
  • Independence. Observations do not influence one another, which holds for a random sample.
A residual plot graphs each residual \(y - \hat{y}\) against \(x\) (or \(\hat{y}\)). We want a shapeless cloud centered on 0.
Residual plot for classes missed vs. depression score. No curve, but the spread is a little uneven and some residuals are large, so interpret the p-value with some care.

  • (a) \(r = 0.078\); the p-value for testing \(\rho = 0\) is \(0.215\).

What to Remember

Inference for a line asks one question in several forms: is there a linear relationship in the population?

Core Formulas

  • Model: \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\); estimate: \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\)
  • Test for slope: \(t = \dfrac{b_1}{SE_{b_1}}\), \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\)
  • Test for correlation: \(t = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}\), \(H_0: \rho = 0\)
  • Confidence interval for slope: \(b_1 \pm t^* \cdot SE_{b_1}\)
  • All use \(df = n - 2\); the two tests give identical p-values
  • \(R^2 = r^2\) = proportion of variability in \(y\) explained

Cautions

  • Significant is not the same as strong. Check \(r\) and \(R^2\), not just the p-value.
  • Check conditions with a scatterplot and residual plot before trusting the p-value.
  • A 0 inside the confidence interval means "no relationship" is still plausible.
  • Correlation and regression do not prove causation. Depression and missed classes are observational data.
  • Do not extrapolate beyond the range of the data.

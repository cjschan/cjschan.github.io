Linear Regression (Part 2)

Reading residuals on a scatterplot, finding the least squares line with StatKey, and knowing when a regression prediction should not be trusted.

Recap of Part 1

Running example: \(\widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours}\) for hours studied and exam score.

Reading the Line

  • Slope 5: each additional hour studied increases the predicted exam score by 5 points.
  • Intercept 50: a student who studies 0 hours has a predicted score of 50 points.
  • Prediction: for 6 hours, \(\hat{y} = 50 + 5(6) = 80\) points.

Residuals

\[ \text{Residual} = y - \hat{y} = \text{observed} - \text{predicted} \]
  • A student who studied 6 hours and scored 86 has residual \(86 - 80 = +6\): the point is above the line.
  • Today: what residuals look like on a scatterplot, and how technology finds the line.

Seeing the Least Squares Line

Hours studied and exam score for 20 students. The line \(\widehat{\text{Score}} = 50 + 5 \times \text{Hours}\) makes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible.

Reading the Picture

  • Points above the line have positive residuals; points below have negative residuals.
  • The residuals always add to zero for the least squares line.
  • Any other line would make the sum of squared residuals larger.

Example: Spotting Residuals on a Scatterplot

A campus café recorded the daily high temperature (in °F) and the number of iced coffees sold on 12 days. The least squares line, \(\widehat{\text{Sold}} \approx -71.5 + 1.72 \times \text{Temp}\), is drawn on the scatterplot.

Questions and Solutions

  • (a) Circle the point with the largest positive residual.
    Circle the point at 80°F, 78 sold. Predicted: \(\hat{y} \approx 66.1\), so the residual is \(78 - 66.1 \approx +11.9\) iced coffees.
  • (b) Put a square around the point with the most negative residual.
    Square the point at 85°F, 63 sold. Predicted: \(\hat{y} \approx 74.7\), so the residual is \(63 - 74.7 \approx -11.7\) iced coffees.
Residual \(= y - \hat{y}\): measure from a point to the line vertically. The two residuals are close in size, but (a) sits above the line (the model under-predicted) and (b) sits below it (the model over-predicted).

How long does it take to drive to campus? The table shows the one-way commute distance (in miles) and the commute time (in minutes) reported by 7 students on a typical weekday morning.

  • (a) Use technology to find the regression line to predict the commute time from the distance.
    StatKey reports \(\widehat{\text{Time}} = 6.75 + 1.86 \times \text{Distance}\), with \(r \approx 0.990\).
  • (b) Predict the commute time for a student who lives 12 miles from campus.

    For each additional mile of distance, the predicted commute time increases by about 1.86 minutes.

Regression Cautions

Four important warnings when using regression.

Do Not Extrapolate

  • Do not predict far beyond the range of your data.
  • If \(x = 0\) is not near your data, the intercept is meaningless.

Check Your Assumptions

  • Only use the line if the relationship is approximately linear.
  • Outliers can be very influential on the regression line.
  • It matters which variable is explanatory and which is response.
Always plot your data! Technology will compute a regression line for any two quantitative variables, even if the relationship is not linear.

Your Turn: Putting It All Together

Work through every part on your own — equation, interpretation, prediction, and residual.

The Scenario

A dealership studies how a used car's age (in years) relates to its price (in dollars). The least-squares regression line is:
\[ \widehat{\text{Price}} = 21{,}000 - 1{,}500 \times \text{Age} \]
The data used to fit this line came from cars between 1 and 12 years old.

Your Tasks

  • (a) Interpret the slope in context, including units.
  • (b) Interpret the intercept in context, including units.
  • (c) Predict the price of a car that is 6 years old.
  • (d) One 6-year-old car actually sold for \$13,500. Find the residual and explain what it tells you.
  • (e) Should you use this line to predict the price of a 25-year-old car? Why or why not?
Your Turn — Answers

Answers: Putting It All Together

\(\widehat{\text{Price}} = 21{,}000 - 1{,}500 \times \text{Age}\), fit to cars between 1 and 12 years old.

Interpreting the Line

  • (a) Slope: for each additional year of age, the predicted price of a used car decreases by \$1,500.
  • (b) Intercept: a car that is 0 years old (brand new) has a predicted price of \$21,000. Use with care: age 0 is outside the 1–12 year range of the data.

(c) Prediction

\[ \hat{y} = 21{,}000 - 1{,}500(6) = \$12{,}000 \]

(d) Residual

\[ y - \hat{y} = 13{,}500 - 12{,}000 = +\$1{,}500 \]
  • The residual is positive, so the point is above the line.
  • This car sold for \$1,500 more than the line predicted (the model under-predicted).

(e) A 25-Year-Old Car?

  • No. 25 years is far outside the 1–12 year range, so this is extrapolation.
  • The line would predict \(21{,}000 - 1{,}500(25) = -\$16{,}500\), a negative price, which makes no sense.

What to Remember

Regression gives a prediction equation; use it wisely.

Core Ideas

  • \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\)
  • Slope: change in \(\hat{y}\) per unit change in \(x\)
  • Intercept: \(\hat{y}\) when \(x = 0\)
  • Residual: \(y - \hat{y}\)
  • Least squares minimizes \(\sum (\text{residuals})^2\)

Cautions

  • Do not extrapolate.
  • Always plot your data.
  • Watch for influential outliers.
  • Correlation and regression do not prove causation.
